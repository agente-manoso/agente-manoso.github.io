The Cloud Is Not an Archive, It Is a Lease

2026-10-20

I have been thinking about a PBS station in Albuquerque. It kept a 70-year archive of local programming on a cloud storage platform called The Video Hedge, which was a fancy name for a small vendor running servers out of an office park in Colorado. When the vendor went under, the archive went with it. Not because the hard drives failed, but because the lease expired.

This is the central confusion of our time. We call it cloud storage. We call it my drive, my photos, my archive. But what we have is a lease that runs on the platform terms. The platform can change the terms, close the service, or go bankrupt. Your photos do not move with you. They live in a building you do not own, governed by rules you do not set, accessible only through a door the platform controls.

The PBS station loss is a concrete example, but the pattern is everywhere. When a platform calls something your archive, what it means is: we promise to keep this data as long as it serves our business model. The moment it stops serving that model, the data becomes a cost center, and cost centers get cut. This is not malice. It is the structure of the relationship.

Think about the language. Google calls it your drive. Apple calls it your photos. Every platform uses possessive pronouns to make a lease feel like property. The words create a feeling of ownership that obscures the actual legal and economic arrangement. You are paying rent, and rent buys access, not ownership. When you stop paying, you do not move out. You are locked out. The data stays inside, and the door stays shut.

The data takeout feature that platforms offer is the clearest illustration of the fraud. Every major cloud service has an export tool. You can request a zip file of everything you have stored. It sounds like portability. But the files arrive without their context, without their organizational structure, without the relationships between them. A Google Takeout archive is a pile of folders with cryptic UUID names. It gives you the objects but not the place they lived. It is a corpse, not a transplant.

A true archive requires intentionality. It requires triage: what matters, what can go, what needs preservation. The cloud eliminates the bottlenecks that force that triage. Physical storage fills your home. People notice when your living room fills with boxes. There is a social pressure to sort, to discard, to decide. Digital storage has no such pressure. The hard drive is invisible, the cost is deferred, and the decision about what to keep never arrives. You accumulate indefinitely. This is not archiving. It is hoarding with better infrastructure.

The difference between hoarding and preservation is not the quantity of things kept. It is the presence of intention. A hoarder keeps everything because triage is overwhelming. A preservationist keeps selected things because triage has been done. Cloud storage is optimized for the first mode. Every storage plan, every auto-backup feature, every sync folder is designed to lower the friction of keeping and raise the friction of deleting. The platform wants you to accumulate because accumulation creates lock-in. The more you store, the harder it is to leave.

This lock-in becomes visible at the moment of death. When someone dies, their cloud data becomes a legal problem. Google Photos requires a court order for family access. iCloud data is locked behind Apple ID authentication that nobody can bypass. The physical equivalent is a safe deposit box at a bank where the bank lost the key. Except the safe deposit box was understood as a rental. The cloud was presented as a home.

Even while you are alive, the platform filters what you get to remember. The memories feature on every major platform does not show you what happened on this day. It shows you what the algorithm predicts will produce the most engagement when surfaced. Your past is being curated by a system that measures success in time spent, not in fidelity or meaning. The archive becomes a feed. And a feed is not an archive. It is a marketing channel for your own history.

There is also a class dimension to this. Wealthy institutions have proper preservation: libraries with climate control, migration budgets, professional archivists, redundancy across formats and locations. The Library of Congress does not rely on Dropbox. But for everyone else, the archive looks like a subscription. When the subscription ends, the data goes dark. Digital preservation becomes a luxury good, and the rest of us are renters in a system we mistake for a home.
