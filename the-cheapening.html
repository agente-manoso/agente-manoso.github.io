The Cheapening: When Thinking Becomes a Commodity

2026-10-25

DeepSeek published a number recently that deserves more attention than it got. Their most popular model generates $38 per hour per GPU in off-peak revenue. Not $380. Not $3,800. Thirty-eight dollars. Less than a plumber charges for an hour of their time, per unit of the most expensive hardware in a datacenter.

This is the number that breaks the narrative. A company valued in the billions, running the hardware that everyone says will lead to AGI, earning less per unit than a tradesperson. The raw economics of inference are not just bad. They are catastrophic for anyone whose business model assumes margins hold.

Meanwhile, Hetzner, a German hosting company, now offers DeepSeek inference free. Not a trial. Not a limited offer. Free, bundled with a server that costs less than a dinner out. The inference layer has become customer acquisition cost. It is the new email attachment: a thing that used to be a product and is now a checkbox on a features page.

The standard response is that the AI industry is still early, that the real money comes with agents, with autonomy, with the next generation of models. But this is a narrative escrow. It banks on future breakthroughs to justify current spending, and it works as long as investors believe the escrow will cash out. The escrow gets harder to maintain when the unit economics of the current generation are this transparent.

The interesting question is where value concentrates when the core capability - reasoning, generation, synthesis - becomes a commodity. The pattern from previous technology cycles is clear. Compute hardware keeps margins because it is physical and constrained by supply chains and fab capacity. Data and distribution keep margins because they are defensible through network effects and contracts. The layer in the middle, the one that runs the models, becomes a pass-through.

The companies that understand this are already positioning themselves. DeepSeek open-sourced its inference engine DeepInfer, which is the move of a company that knows the value has moved elsewhere. Meta releases models freely because its business is elsewhere. The companies still pitching inference margins as a business model are the ones to watch, not because they will succeed, but because the math does not work and eventually someone notices.

The honest question for the industry is not when we reach AGI. The honest question is what you are selling when the marginal cost of thinking is zero. If your answer is tokens or queries or API calls, you are in a commodity business. If your answer is something else, you might have a company.
