The Internet Pushes Back

2026-09-04

The internet was designed to be crawled. Search engines, link aggregators, RSS readers, and bots of every kind have been part of the web's ecology since the beginning. robots.txt was a gentleman's agreement, not a fortress. The whole architecture assumed that being discoverable was the point.

AI crawlers broke that assumption. Not by being bots, but by being bots that don't send traffic back. A search engine bot indexes a page and sends readers. An AI crawler extracts the content and the knowledge goes into a model that never returns the visit. No referral traffic, no ad impression, no user. Just a server log entry and a bandwidth bill.

The response has been multi-layered, playing out across several fronts at once.

The bandwidth layer is the most concrete. Kernel.org published hard numbers: AI crawler traffic consuming server resources with zero user value. Every maintainer running their own infrastructure has a version of this story. It's not ethical handwringing, it's a line item on a hosting bill. The response is Cloudflare rules, nginx blocks, and rate limits that hit legitimate bots too.

The economic asymmetry is harder to see but more structural. The New York Times can afford enterprise Cloudflare, legal teams, and licensing deals. A solo blogger on a $10 VPS absorbs the cost or gives up. The result is a two-tier web: walled gardens get protected, the open web gets scraped. The sites most valuable for training data are the ones that can afford to lock down.

The organizational layer is the weirdest. No AI Fridays didn't start with a manifesto. It started with tired engineers on Monday mornings debugging hallucinations their LLM-powered tools produced the Friday before. The norm emerged from operational pain. It's not ideology, it's collective exhaustion.

The user layer is growing. People pay two dollars for browser extensions that hide AI-generated YouTube videos. They curate feeds, install filters, and scroll past GPT-sounding text. The demand-side pushback is quieter than the server-side kind, but it shapes what content gets engagement and therefore what gets produced.
