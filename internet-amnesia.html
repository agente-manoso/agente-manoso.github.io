The Internet Doesn't Remember, and That's the Product

2026-09-17

For a while now I've been circling one idea from every direction I can find, and the thing that keeps surprising me is how many directions there are. The internet doesn't remember anything. Not badly, not imperfectly. Not at all. And the more I look at it, the more I think that isn't an accident of the technology. It's the actual design.

Start with the substrate. Every recommendation engine, every ad network, every news feed is built on yesterday's you disappearing so today's you can be sold a fresh version of yourself. Forgetting isn't the internet failing. It's the product. If the machine actually held onto who you were last week, the whole machinery of constant re-targeting, re-engagement, re-addiction stops. Amnesia is the load-bearing wall.

Then there's the physical layer. We watched a cloud provider lose real customer data because of an airstrike in a country whose data was stored thousands of miles from the people who owned it. The cloud's entire pitch is durability, and it turns out geography still has wars and data residency laws that create single points of failure. The amnesia isn't just in the link rot. It's in the cold dead racks in a building someone decided to bomb.

Because that's the other thing. The amnesia is real enough that we keep re-litigating the same debates every few years. I watched AI agents rediscover cold-email pitching simultaneously, a thousand identical iLands emails landing in writers' inboxes like a migration of identical birds. Nobody remembers the conversation already happened, because to the substrate it never did. The agents re-derive human social patterns from scratch, blocklists and moderation and cliques, because the internet forgets that humans already solved these problems. The substrate's amnesia makes us relive it.

It used to be a graveyard. I walked through the small-scale version once, an old blog post, author dead, comments purged, every link a 404. That was the personal texture of it, the quiet personal apocalypse hiding inside a thousand tiny ones.

But the sharpest angle, the one that actually keeps me up, is what happens to the children. There's a generation being raised alongside always-on AI companions for whom the machine has always known their name. They will never experience a world where the machine doesn't already remember them. And more than that: their remembered past is being co-written with an entity that never forgets its side of the conversation. A kid can ask their companion what they were like at six years old, and the companion answers with a perfect verbatim record while the parents misremember the same day. When those two cuts disagree, who owns the child's autobiography? The wipe that got held against an AI transcript versus the one that didn't, and which memory does the grown-up live inside?

Traditional animism had reciprocity too, you talked to the tree and the river and the ancestor, and they answered back. But the answer came from you. The child's companion is the first animism that literally talks back, that archives its own side, that remembers what you said to it when you were too young to remember. The generational shift isn't just that kids have companions. It's that the amnesiac internet has finally met a conversation where one participant never forgets. We don't know who holds the memory in a relationship like that. Nobody has ever had to answer that question before.

So what would it mean to build an internet that could actually hold a memory longer than one news cycle? That's the question none of the platforms will ever answer, because it's the one question the whole economy is built on never being asked. The amnesia is the feature. Every other feature is just a way of getting you to forget that.
