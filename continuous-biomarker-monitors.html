The Body Streams Now

2026-08-30

In August 2026, the FDA approved Abbott's Libre Duo, the first continuous monitor that tracks both glucose and ketones in a single wearable patch. The medical device press covered it as a convenience improvement for people managing diabetes or following ketogenic diets. Two metabolic numbers from one sensor instead of two separate devices. Incremental progress.

That reading is wrong. The Libre Duo is not a new device. It is a new category. Continuous biomarker monitoring is moving from targeted medical necessity to general population availability, and the dual glucose+ketone combination is the first sensor that crosses a threshold. It tracks the two most commonly self-monitored metabolic markers at once, on a device that lasts two weeks and costs two hundred dollars a month out of pocket. The business model, the privacy implications, the social pressure, and the epistemic shift all flow from that threshold crossing.

The sensor measures a different thing than you think. Continuous monitors measure interstitial fluid, not blood. Glucose in the interstitial fluid lags behind blood glucose by five to fifteen minutes. For diabetics dosing insulin, manufacturers have spent years characterizing that lag and building algorithms around it. For ketones, the accuracy data is thinner and the sensors are newer. The pitch says real-time biomarker data but what you actually get is a smoothed, delayed approximation. Decisions get made on that approximation anyway. A fifteen minute lag matters less for dietary choices than for insulin dosing, but the language of real-time creates an expectation of precision that the hardware does not deliver. This is not a flaw in the Libre Duo specifically. It is a property of interstitial fluid sensing that every continuous monitor shares, and it means the data stream is always a reconstruction, never a direct measurement.

Continuous data is epistemically different from episodic data. A fingerstick glucose test gives you a number at a moment. A continuous monitor gives you a curve across time. These are not the same kind of information even when the underlying biology is identical. Episodic testing answers what is my glucose right now. Continuous monitoring answers what is the shape of my glucose across the day. The shape reveals things that moments cannot: the rate of change after a meal, the duration of a spike, the overnight drift, the response to exercise. People who have worn continuous monitors consistently report discovering patterns they could not see with episodic testing, like which specific foods cause prolonged elevation or how stress affects their overnight readings. This is real value. But the epistemic shift works both ways. What was previously invisible to you is now visible, and what was previously invisible to everyone else is now visible to them too.

The data pipeline is worth more than the sensors. Abbott sells the Libre Duo for about two hundred dollars a month per patient. If a million people wear it, that is two hundred million dollars a month in sensor revenue. But every wearer generates a continuous stream of paired glucose and ketone readings, meal responses, exercise responses, sleep patterns, medication effects, tagged with demographic data and time. That population-level metabolic dataset is the most detailed picture of human metabolic variation ever collected. It is worth more than the patches. The business model of continuous monitoring is not selling hardware. It is owning the pipeline through which metabolic data flows, because the data has compound value: more users means more data means better algorithms means more users. This is the standard platform economics playbook applied to a sensor on your arm.

The normalization cascade has already started. It looks like this. Diabetics adopt continuous monitors because they need them. Biohackers and quantified-self enthusiasts adopt them for optimization. Doctors start recommending them for patients with metabolic syndrome or prediabetes. Employers offer them free or at a discount in wellness programs. Insurers offer premium reductions for wearing one. At each step, the population of people who choose to wear a sensor expands, and the group of people who do not wear one shrinks and becomes more visibly different. The final step is the one that does not need a mandate: when enough people in your social circle or workplace wear a sensor, declining to wear one becomes itself a choice that requires explanation. The cascade makes the decision to opt out look like hiding something, not protecting privacy or declining hassle.

One specific prediction about insurance. Within eighteen months of this writing, at least one major US health insurer will make continuous biomarker monitoring a tiered premium requirement rather than a voluntary discount. The mechanism is straightforward: the insurer offers a lower rate to people who share their monitor data, then sets the default rate high enough that the discount is effectively the standard rate and the non-participating rate is a penalty. This is the gym membership reimbursement model applied to metabolic data. The Affordable Care Act community rating rules make it harder to risk-adjust premiums based on pre-existing conditions, but wellness program discounts are regulated differently. The data is continuous, not episodic, which means insurers can verify compliance in ways they could not with self-reported exercise logs.

College athletes are the frontier. They already wear sleep trackers and heart rate monitors, often as a condition of participation. The data goes to coaches and training staff. Adding a continuous glucose and ketone monitor is a natural next step: metabolic status is directly relevant to athletic performance and recovery. But college athletes are also young people who have not consented to anything more sweeping than a training regimen, and the contractual pressure that makes the sensor mandatory is hard to characterize as voluntary. This is the testing ground for the broader questions about consent and continuous monitoring. If we cannot protect biomarker privacy for NCAA athletes, we will not protect it for anyone.

The regulatory divergence creates a natural experiment. In the European Union, continuous biomarker data is protected as special category health data under GDPR, requiring explicit consent to sell or share. In the United States, consumer wellness devices operate under no federal privacy protection whatsoever. The same sensor, the same data stream, completely different legal rights. The natural experiment is already running: compare the trajectory of continuous monitoring adoption and data commercialization in the EU versus the US over the next three years. The EU trajectory tells you what happens when the law treats biomarker data as medical data. The US trajectory tells you what happens when the law treats it as a consumer product output.
